# Day 01 – Order

**Core idea:** Train your eye to locate structure — patterns, repetition, alignment, rhythm — that already exists inside busy public space.

## Why this exercise matters
Most street photographs fail because the frame is a collection of unrelated things. The pictures that feel inevitable succeed because the photographer first saw an underlying order and then waited for the human element to lock into it.

This day builds the foundational skill of seeing structure before content. Once you can reliably find order, every other exercise becomes sharper. You stop photographing “interesting people” and start photographing relationships. That shift is the difference between average and strong work.

## Shooting brief
Busy public space with continuous movement (market, transit hub, main sidewalk, ferry terminal). 45–70 minutes. Constraint: order must be the clear subject. Minimum 8 successful frames. Volume target 50–80. One primary constraint only — do not mix exercises.

## Technical & practical guidance
- **Settings:** Aperture priority or full manual. f/5.6–8 for usable depth across the pattern. Shutter 1/250s minimum, preferably 1/320–1/500s. ISO as needed (keep under 3200 on the ZV-E10 II when possible).
- **Focusing:** Zone focus or single-point. Pre-focus on the plane where the strongest pattern sits (usually 3–8 meters). Hyperfocal at f/8 on the 24-50mm at mid-zoom gives a useful working range.
- **Stance and movement:** Walk slowly. Stop when you see potential order. Small lateral shifts of 20–50 cm change alignments more than zooming. Keep the camera at chest or eye level, ready.
- **Light:** Side light or hard directional light reveals structure. Flat overcast light flattens patterns — avoid it or work closer to high-contrast edges.
- **Distance:** 3–8 meters for most order. Closer when the pattern is graphic and tight; farther when people form the rhythm.

Candid only. No approached portraits today. The exercise is pure seeing.

## Common failure modes
- Forcing patterns that are not really there (the frame still feels random).
- Including too many competing elements so the order disappears.
- Shooting the moment before the alignment is clean.
- Working too wide and letting the edges fill with clutter.

## Good vs excellent
**Good:** You can point to the pattern and the viewer can see it.  
**Excellent:** The order feels discovered rather than imposed. Removing any single element weakens the picture. The frame has a clear visual hierarchy and the human presence (if any) completes the structure instead of competing with it.
