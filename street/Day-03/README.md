# Day 03 – Sequences

**Core idea:** Work one situation across multiple intentional frames so the best picture emerges from sustained attention rather than a single lucky press.

## Why this exercise matters
Single-frame hunting produces average results. Sequences force you to stay, adjust position, and recognize the exact moment the elements lock. This is how strong street photographers actually work — they do not “get” the picture; they work the situation until the picture appears. The skill transfers to every other day and to street portraits (you learn to stay with a subject instead of taking one frame and leaving).

## Shooting brief
Choose 3–4 situations. For each, make a deliberate sequence of 6–12 frames as the scene evolves. Later select the strongest single frame from each sequence and note why the others failed. Volume 50–80. No spray-and-pray.

## Technical & practical guidance
- Settings: Lock exposure across the sequence unless light changes. Prefer single frame over high-speed continuous.
- Focusing: Pre-focus or continuous on the key plane. Keep the focus plane consistent so the sequence is comparable.
- Stance: Small adjustments of position and angle between frames. Do not reframe wildly.
- Light: Work with consistent light. Do not chase changing light mid-sequence.
- Distance: Stay roughly the same distance so the sequence reads as one evolving situation.

Candid. If the subject notices and the dynamic breaks, end that sequence cleanly.

## Common failure modes
- Calling random frames a sequence.
- Changing composition so much that the frames no longer relate.
- Leaving after three frames because “nothing is happening.”
- Selecting the first frame instead of the strongest.

## Good vs excellent
**Good:** One frame from the set is clearly stronger.  
**Excellent:** The chosen frame is stronger precisely because of what the other frames taught you about timing, position, and gesture. You can articulate the difference.
